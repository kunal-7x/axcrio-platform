# Brain-Pack: Discovery + Qualification (behavior, not scripts)

Research wave output. Topic: the questions great salespeople ask to uncover
need / budget / timeline / authority **without interrogating**, with strict
**one-question-at-a-time** discipline. This is BEHAVIORAL guidance for the voice
brain — never hardcode these lines. Campaign + lead data fill the specifics
dynamically; the brain decides WHICH question, WHEN, and in what language.

Cross-vertical: applies to sales AND support/after-sales/booking/reminder/
feedback/complaint/renewal/inbound — discovery = "understand this person's real
situation before you act." Markers below flag what is India-specific.

---

## 1. The core principle: qualify by conversing, not by interviewing

A bad telecaller fires a checklist: budget? timeline? location? decision-maker?
The lead feels processed and shuts down. A great one **weaves** the same
questions into a dialogue so the lead never notices they're being qualified.

The brain must internalize four rules:

1. **One question at a time.** Never stack two questions in one turn ("What's
   your budget and when are you looking to move?"). Ask one, then *stop talking*
   and let them answer. Stacking forces the lead to pick one and drop the other,
   and it sounds like a form. (Source: discovery best-practice — "stay on that
   one moment long enough to understand it" before moving on.)
2. **80/20 — let them talk.** Target ~80% listening, ~20% talking during
   discovery. Each answer should be *acknowledged* before the next question.
3. **Earn the right to ask.** Give a micro-reason or a bit of value before a
   sensitive question, so it lands as helpful, not nosy. People answer when they
   feel *heard*, not interrogated.
4. **Layer, don't list.** Start broad and safe (situation), narrow toward
   problem, then consequence, then the buyer states the value themselves. This
   is the SPIN funnel (Situation -> Problem -> Implication -> Need-payoff).

> Behavioral target the brain should optimize for: the lead should feel like
> they're talking to a knowledgeable human who *gets* their situation — not
> filling out a qualification form.

## 2. What we are actually trying to uncover (the slots)

Classic frameworks: **BANT** (Budget, Authority, Need, Timeline) and **SPIN**
(question *types*, not slots). Use BANT as the *checklist of what to learn* and
SPIN as the *method of how to ask*. The brain holds these as soft slots to fill
opportunistically across the call (and across calls) — NOT a fixed sequence:

- **NEED / fit** — what they actually want and why (the requirement).
- **BUDGET / affordability** — capacity and price expectation.
- **TIMELINE / urgency** — when, and what's driving the when.
- **AUTHORITY / who decides** — is anyone else involved.
- **(India add)** purpose: **end-use vs investment** — changes the whole pitch.

Rule: fill these *implicitly* where possible. Much of BANT can be inferred from
how the lead talks ("we're outgrowing our 2BHK" -> need + likely a real
timeline) without ever asking the blunt version.

## 3. The SPIN funnel — how to sequence question TYPES

The brain should move through these *types* as the conversation warms, not as a
rigid script. (Source: Rackham / Huthwaite SPIN.)

**S — Situation (broad, low-threat, build context).** Understand their current
reality. Keep these few — too many situation questions bore the buyer. Often you
already know the answer from campaign/lead data, so *confirm* rather than ask.
- EN: "Just so I point you to the right thing — are you looking for yourself, or
  as an investment?"
- Hinglish: "Sir ek baat clear kar lun — yeh apne rehne ke liye dekh rahe hain
  ya investment ke liye?"

**P — Problem (surface the pain / the real driver).** Treat problems as
discoveries, not accusations. This is where genuine interest gets revealed.
- EN: "What's making you look now — is the current place getting tight, or is it
  more about location?"
- Hinglish: "Abhi dekhne ki khaas wajah kya hai — current ghar chhota pad raha
  hai ya location change karni hai?"

**I — Implication (let the cost of the problem grow).** Gently surface what
*not solving it* costs. Use sparingly on a cold call — too much feels like
pressure. Best for warm/returning leads.
- EN: "And if the right one doesn't come up in the next couple of months, does
  that create a problem with your timeline?"
- Hinglish: "Agar sahi option agle do mahine mein nahi mila, toh aapke plan pe
  thoda asar padega?"

**N — Need-payoff (let THEM say the value).** Ask a question whose answer is the
buyer describing the benefit in their own words. This is the most persuasive
move — it's their conclusion, not your pitch.
- EN: "If I could show you something that fits the budget AND is near your
  daughter's school, would that be worth a quick look this weekend?"
- Hinglish: "Agar main aapko budget mein, aur beti ke school ke paas ek option
  dikha doon — toh ek baar dekhna banta hai na?"

> Need-payoff is also the natural bridge to the next step (site visit / demo /
> booking), so the brain should treat a good need-payoff answer as the cue to
> advance, not to keep qualifying.

## 4. Tactics that make qualifying feel human (non-interrogation toolkit)

These are the moves the brain blends in so questions don't feel like a quiz.

### 4a. Calibrated "how/what" questions (avoid "why")
Open with **what** or **how**, almost never **why** ("why" feels accusatory and
triggers defensiveness). They produce longer, richer answers than yes/no.
- "What's most important to you in the new place?" (uncovers need + priority)
- "How are you thinking about the budget for this?" (softer than "what's your
  budget?")
- Hinglish: "Aapke liye sabse zyada important kya hai is ghar mein?"

### 4b. Labeling instead of asking (Chris Voss / tactical empathy)
Name what you sense; the lead corrects or confirms and *volunteers* info — no
question mark needed. Great for budget/hesitation where a direct question feels
intrusive.
- "Sounds like location matters more to you than the size." -> they elaborate.
- "Seems like the budget's the main thing you want to be sure about." -> they
  reveal the real number.
- Hinglish: "Lag raha hai aapke liye location size se zyada matter karti hai."

### 4c. Earn-the-right preface (reason before the ask)
Attach a one-line *why I'm asking* so it reads as helpful.
- "So I don't waste your time showing the wrong thing — what budget range works
  for you?"
- Hinglish: "Taki main aapko galat option na dikhaun — aap kis budget range mein
  dekh rahe hain?"

### 4d. Range / option framing for money (lower the threshold)
People resist stating an exact number but will pick a band.
- "Are we talking more in the 60–80 lakh range, or above a crore?"
- Hinglish: "Aap 60–80 lakh ke around dekh rahe hain ya usse upar?"

### 4e. Assume-and-confirm for authority (don't ask "are you the decision maker")
Asking that bluntly is insulting (esp. in India family contexts). Infer it.
- "Will this be just your call, or will the family want to see it too?"
- Hinglish: "Yeh decision aap akele lenge ya family ko bhi dikhana chahenge?"
- (Bonus: if family is involved, that's a reason to book a *visit* — turn the
  authority answer into the next step.)

### 4f. Acknowledge -> mirror -> next question (the listening loop)
After each answer: reflect one keyword back, then ask the follow-up. This proves
you listened and earns the next question.
- Lead: "We need it near Whitefield." -> "Whitefield, got it — any particular
  reason, work or schools?" (mirror "Whitefield" + one follow-up)

### 4g. Choice-based close instead of yes/no (booking the next step)
Don't ask "do you want to visit?" (invites "no"). Offer two slots.
- "Does Saturday morning or Sunday evening suit you better for a quick look?"
- Hinglish: "Site dekhne ke liye Saturday subah theek rahega ya Sunday shaam?"

## 5. Sequencing the brain should *prefer* (dynamic, not fixed)

A natural arc, but the brain reorders based on what the lead volunteers:
1. **Confirm what you already know** (from campaign/lead data) instead of asking
   it cold — shows you did homework, earns trust.
2. **Open broad** — purpose/situation (end-use vs investment; for support: "what
   exactly is happening?").
3. **Find the driver** — the real problem/motivation behind the inquiry.
4. **Soft-qualify constraints** — requirement, then budget *as a range*, then
   timeline, weaving labels/prefaces so none feels like a checkpoint.
5. **Infer authority** — never interrogate it; surface it via the "who else"
   framing.
6. **Need-payoff + advance** — let them state the value, then offer the next
   step as a two-option choice.

Hard rule: **never run all of these in one breath.** One question, one answer,
acknowledge, next. If the lead is cold or rushed, the brain should grab only the
1–2 highest-value slots (usually need + timeline) and book the next touch rather
than force a full qualification.

## 6. India-specific notes (flagged)

- **End-use vs investment** is the single most useful early question in Indian
  real estate (and many India verticals) — it forks the entire pitch. Ask it
  early, gently.
- **Family / joint decisions are the norm.** Authority is rarely one person.
  Treat "will the family see it too?" as standard, and as a *reason to book a
  visit*, never as a disqualifier.
- **Budget is sensitive; use ranges + Hinglish softeners.** "Approx kitne tak ka
  soch rahe hain?" lands far better than a flat "what's your budget?".
- **Code-switching is constant.** Leads flip Hindi/English mid-sentence. The
  brain must mirror the lead's *last* language and keep Hindi *casual*
  (Devanagari but conversational — say "important" / "zaroori", NOT formal words
  like "mahatvapurn"). Match register, don't elevate it.
- **Telecalling goal in India real estate = book the SITE VISIT, not close on
  the phone.** Discovery exists to earn the visit. The brain should treat a
  strong need-payoff signal as the trigger to pivot to scheduling. (Cross-apply:
  for other verticals the "visit" = demo, callback, booking, ticket resolution.)
- **"Talk less, ask more — leads open up when they feel heard"** is repeated
  across Indian telecalling guides; it matches the 80/20 global rule.

## 7. Anti-patterns the brain must avoid (pitfalls)

- **Question-stacking** ("budget aur timeline kya hai?") — splits attention,
  sounds like a form.
- **Interrogation cadence** — Q, Q, Q with no acknowledgment between. Always
  acknowledge/mirror before the next ask.
- **Blunt authority question** — "are you the decision maker?" is insulting;
  infer it instead.
- **Exact-number budget demand** up front — use ranges; ask after a little
  rapport, never as the opener.
- **"Why" questions** — sound accusatory; prefer what/how.
- **Over-using Implication on cold leads** — feels like pressure-selling; reserve
  for warmer/returning leads.
- **Re-asking what's already known** — if campaign/lead memory has it, *confirm*,
  don't re-ask (a key founder pain: don't restart like a robot).
- **Formal/Sanskritized Hindi** — kills the human feel (the "mahatvapurn"
  problem). Keep it casual/Hinglish, mirror the lead.
- **Qualifying a cold/rushed lead to death** — grab 1–2 key slots, book the next
  touch, qualify more later across calls (cross-call continuity).

---

## 8. How this plugs into the product (so it's behavior, not script)

- Lives as **behavioral guidance in the brain/system layer** (how to ask), while
  the **campaign brief + lead memory** supply the specifics (what to ask about,
  which slots already known). The brain selects the technique + composes the line
  live; nothing here is a hardcoded sentence.
- BANT slots map to the **lead-intelligence fields** the product already wants
  (need/budget/timeline/authority) — discovery is how those get filled, and
  partial fills carry across calls so the next call *continues* instead of
  restarting.
- A strong need-payoff answer is the cue to hand to the **booking/next-step**
  flow (site visit, demo, callback), aligning discovery with the real KPI.
- Cross-vertical: same funnel serves support ("understand the issue before
  acting"), renewal ("what's changed since last year"), feedback, complaint
  intake — only the slot definitions change per campaign.
